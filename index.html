03 / Grammar

Three tools for moving through time

Lesson 23 tells past, present, and future events in a single short letter. These three structures keep the order clear. A fourth tool, prepositions, shows where each event happens and who is involved.

Tool 01 / Past perfect

The earlier past

had + past participle

Use the past perfect for an action that happened before another past action or moment.

Work on it had begun before my sister left.

Why it matters: Both actions are in the past. Had begun tells us which one came first.

Tool 02 / Reported speech

Words carried forward

said / told + (that) + would / could

When the reporting verb is past, the modal often moves back: will becomes would, and can becomes could.

In her letter, she said that she would come to England next year.

In my letter, I told her that she could stay with us.

Why it matters: The visit is still in the future, but the report is anchored in the past.

Tool 03 / First conditional

A real possibility

If + present simple, ... will + base verb

Use this pattern for a possible future situation and its result. Keep will out of the if clause.

If she comes, she will get a surprise.

Why it matters: The visit is not certain yet. The surprise depends on the condition.

Common trap

Two different "had"s

Do not confuse a past-perfect auxiliary with the main verb had.

Main verb: simple pastI had a letter from my sister yesterday.
Auxiliary: past perfectWork on it had begun before my sister left.

How the letter fits together

Past report, future plan

The letter was written in the past, but the sister's plan points to next year. Reported speech and the first conditional can sit together in one short narrative.

past report → would come → if she comes → she will get

Remember: would is the past form used in the report; will appears in the open future result. Do not use would in the if clause.

05 / Pronunciation

Sound like the story

This lesson is full of long words, final consonant clusters, and one very important name. Listen, copy, then say the lines with the same rhythm.

Sound focus 01

/dʒ/

Voiced

The buzzing j sound

In Nigeria, strange, and large, the letters make a single voiced sound: /dʒ/. Put the tip of your tongue behind the ridge, add voice, and release. Do not split it into two heavy sounds.

Sound focus 02

/ŋɡ/

Nasal + release

The middle of England

Say England as ING-gluhnd, not with a long ee at the start. The first syllable ends with the nasal /ŋ/; the /ɡ/ begins the next part. Keep the first vowel short.

Sound focus 03

/nθs/ · /str/

Final clusters

Do not lose the ending

English often packs consonants together at the end of a word. Start slowly, keep every part, then speed up without adding an extra vowel.

Count the syllables: months = 1, strange = 1, district = 2. Keep the final consonants attached to the vowel before them.

Chunking drill

Read in small groups, not one word at a time

A single slash marks a short pause. A double slash marks a longer pause at the end of a sentence. Keep the words inside each group connected.

She lives in Nigeria. //
In her letter, / she said that she would come to England / next year. //
If she comes, / she will get a surprise. //

Then repeat without the audio. Let your voice rise and fall with the sentence, not with every word.

    07 / Writing

    Write your own news

    Now use the lesson's patterns in a letter of your own. You will describe a new home, reach back to an earlier past, report someone's words, and invite a friend with a first conditional.

    Writing task

    A letter about a new home

    You have moved into a new home. Write a letter or email to a friend in 100–150 words. Tell the story of the move and invite your friend to visit.

    1. 1Explain when you received your friend's last message and how you felt.
    2. 2Describe where you live now and two details of the home or garden.
    3. 3Include an earlier past action with the past perfect.
    4. 4Report something a family member said, using said/told + would/could.
    5. 5End with a first conditional invitation: If you come, ... will ...
    Target length 100–150 words

    Shape of the letter

    • Greeting: Dear ... ,
    • Opening: respond to their last message.
    • Body: describe the new place and add the earlier past.
    • News: report a family member's words.
    • Closing: use a conditional invitation and a sign-off.

    Model letter

    Dear Maya,

    I had a letter from you last week, and I was very happy to hear your news. We have just moved into a new house in the country. It is a small, modern house with three bedrooms and a bright kitchen. There is a lovely garden, and I have already planted some flowers.

    Before we moved, we had lived in a flat in the city for six years. My sister told me that she would visit us in the summer. We are now living near a quiet park, and there is a good cafe around the corner.

    If you come in August, you will stay in the spare room, and we can walk to the park together. Please write back and tell me when you can visit.

    Best wishes,
    Ella

    Notice the pattern: the past perfect (had lived) sets up the earlier home, the report uses told ... would, and the conditional invitation uses If ... come, ... will stay. This is one possible letter, not a script to copy.
